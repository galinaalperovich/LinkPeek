How passkeys actually work

By Nadia Okafor

A password is a secret you share with a website, which means the website can lose it. A passkey removes the shared secret. When you create one, your device generates a pair of keys: a private key that never leaves the device, and a public key that the website stores. The public key is safe to publish. It can check a signature, but it cannot make one.

What happens when you sign in

The website sends your device a random challenge. Your device asks you to unlock it with a fingerprint, your face or a PIN, then signs the challenge with the private key and sends the signature back. The website checks it against the public key it has on file. Nothing reusable crosses the network, and the fingerprint or face check never leaves the device.

Why phishing stops working

Each passkey is tied to the exact domain it was created for. Your device will not offer the passkey for your bank to a look-alike address, however convincing the page looks. There is also nothing to type, so there is nothing for a fake login form to capture. A breach of the website's database exposes only public keys, which are useless to an attacker.

Using them on more than one device

  • Apple, Google and most password managers sync passkeys across your devices with end-to-end encryption.
  • To sign in on a computer you do not own, you can scan a QR code and approve the login on your phone.
  • Hardware security keys can hold passkeys that are never synced at all.

The rough edges

Moving passkeys from one ecosystem to another is still clumsy, and account recovery depends on each service. Until that improves, keep a second way to sign in on the accounts that matter most.
