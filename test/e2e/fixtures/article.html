How a sourdough starter actually works

By Mara Lindqvist

A sourdough starter is not an ingredient so much as a small, stable ecosystem. Mix flour and water, wait, and two kinds of microbes that were already on the grain take over: wild yeast, which produces the carbon dioxide that raises the loaf, and lactic acid bacteria, which produce the acids that give sourdough its flavour and make the jar hostile to everything else.

The first ten days

Most new starters bubble vigorously on day two or three. That early burst comes from bacteria that will not survive, and when they die off around day four or five the jar goes quiet. This is the point where most people assume the starter is dead and throw it away. It is not dead. Keep feeding, and by day seven to ten the yeast and the acid-tolerant bacteria settle into a routine: the starter doubles within four to six hours of a feed.

How to feed it

  • Feed once a day at a 1:1:1 ratio of starter, flour and water by weight.
  • Keep it at about 24 °C. Colder slows it down, warmer makes it more sour.
  • Use unbleached flour. A spoonful of whole rye speeds things up.

Keeping it alive

Once established, a starter is hard to kill. It keeps in the fridge on a single feed a week, and a neglected jar usually recovers after two or three feeds at room temperature. A layer of grey liquid on top is alcohol from hungry yeast, not spoilage: stir it in or pour it off.
